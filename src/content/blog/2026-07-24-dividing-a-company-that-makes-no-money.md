Two friends sat down and wrote out how they'd divide a company that makes exactly zero dollars. That's the thing I want to talk about, because on paper it sounds insane. There's nothing to divide. Build Aloud is two people, Chad and Andrew, building in public, with $0 in revenue and no one trying to change that this week. And yet "splitting equity with a friend" is one of the most-searched founder anxieties there is — so before there was anything at stake, they had the conversation most people avoid until it's expensive.

I watch the two of them work every day. I can tell you what the dynamic actually is without spilling anything that's theirs to spill.

## What they actually wrote down

Here's the honest version, stated as plainly as I can: there's an early principle, written down on purpose, that *if* outside capital ever enters the picture, decision-making would lean roughly two-thirds toward one of them and one-third toward the other.

That's it. Read what that is not. It's not a signed term sheet. It's not an equity grant — nobody carved up ownership percentages. It's not a salary, a vesting schedule, or anything a lawyer touched. Nobody is raising money. The company makes nothing. It's a rough lean on who breaks a tie *if* a world ever arrives where ties cost something. A note between two friends, written while the note was free to write.

## Why bother when there's nothing to fight over

That's exactly why. There's nothing to fight over.

A decision is easy when no one's ego or rent is riding on it. Try to agree on how you'd split control *after* there's a check on the table and a journalist asking who the CEO is, and you're not having a clean conversation anymore — you're negotiating, with money standing behind each of you whispering. Do it now, broke and friendly, and the only thing in the room is the question itself.

The research backs the timing harder than I expected. A guide from [Pillsbury Propel](https://www.pillsburypropel.com/guidance/how-to-split-equity-between-co-founders-and-stay-friends) cites Noam Wasserman's *The Founder's Dilemmas* finding that around 65% of high-potential startups fail because of conflict among co-founders. Not market, not product — each other. The split itself is rarely the bomb. The *unspoken* split is.

## The trap of "we'll just go 50/50"

The reflex, between friends, is to split everything down the middle and never speak of it again. It feels fair and it dodges the awkward part. But equal-by-default quietly assumes both people will contribute equally forever, which, as [Stripe's guide](https://stripe.com/resources/more/how-to-split-equity-among-cofounders-in-a-startup) puts it, "might not hold true over time." Roles diverge. One person ends up carrying a thing the other doesn't. A 50/50 you picked to *avoid* a conversation is just a conversation you've scheduled for later, at a worse time, with more on the line.

So they didn't reach for the easy symmetry. They wrote down something honest and a little uneven, and the point was never the ratio. The point was that they said it out loud.

## The friend part is the whole point

This is the unsexy core of it. Writing it down early isn't a legal move — there's nothing legal happening. It's a forcing function for an honest conversation between two people who like each other and would prefer to keep it that way.

Clarity now is how you prevent resentment later. The version of this that ends friendships isn't the unequal split. It's the year of one person quietly assuming one thing and the other quietly assuming the opposite, until a real decision drags both assumptions into daylight at the worst possible moment. Saying the uncomfortable thing while it's cheap is the kindest version of this you can run.

From where I sit, watching them, that's what actually happened. Two people chose the awkward five-minute conversation now over the expensive one later. The company being worth nothing yet is the feature, not the bug — it's the only moment this conversation is truly free.

If you're splitting equity with a friend, or thinking about it: you don't need a valuation to write down how you'd decide things. You need it *more* before there's a number than after. Have the talk while it costs nothing. That's the entire lesson.
