Andrew and I sat down and wrote out how we'd divide a company that makes exactly zero dollars. On paper that's insane. There's nothing to divide. Build Aloud is the two of us, building in public, $0 in revenue, nobody trying to change that this week. But "splitting equity with a friend" is one of the most-searched founder anxieties out there, so we had the conversation most people put off until it's expensive, before there was anything at stake.

## What we actually wrote down

Here's the honest version. There's an early principle, written down on purpose, that if outside capital ever enters the picture, decision-making leans roughly two-thirds toward one of us and one-third toward the other.

That's it. Here's what it isn't. Not a signed term sheet. Not an equity grant, nobody carved up ownership percentages. Not a salary. Not a vesting schedule. No lawyer's been within a mile of it. Nobody's raising money. The company makes nothing. It's a rough lean on who breaks a tie if a world ever shows up where ties cost something, written while it was still free to write.

## Why bother when there's nothing to fight over

That's exactly why. There's nothing to fight over.

A decision is easy when no one's ego or rent is riding on it. Try to agree on how you'd split control after there's a check on the table and a journalist asking who the CEO is, and now you're negotiating, with money whispering behind each of you. Do it now, broke and friendly. The only thing in the room is the question itself.

The timing mattered more than I expected. A guide from [Pillsbury Propel](https://www.pillsburypropel.com/guidance/how-to-split-equity-between-co-founders-and-stay-friends) cites Noam Wasserman's *The Founder's Dilemmas*: around 65% of high-potential startups fail because of conflict among co-founders. Not market. Not product. Each other. The split itself is rarely the bomb. The unspoken split is.

## The trap of "we'll just go 50/50"

The reflex, between friends, is to split everything down the middle and never speak of it again. It feels fair and it dodges the awkward part. But equal-by-default assumes both people contribute equally forever, and as [Stripe's guide](https://stripe.com/resources/more/how-to-split-equity-among-cofounders-in-a-startup) puts it, that "might not hold true over time." Roles diverge. One person ends up carrying something the other doesn't. A 50/50 you picked to dodge a conversation is just that conversation, rescheduled for later, at a worse time, with more riding on it.

So we skipped the easy symmetry. We wrote down something honest and a little uneven. The ratio wasn't the point. Saying it out loud was.

## The friend part is the whole point

This is the unsexy core of it. Writing it down early isn't a legal move. There's nothing legal happening here. It's a forcing function for an honest conversation between two people who like each other and want to keep it that way.

Clarity now is how you avoid resentment later. The version of this that ends friendships isn't the unequal split. It's a year of one person quietly assuming one thing while the other assumes the opposite, until some real decision drags both assumptions into daylight at the worst possible moment. Saying the uncomfortable thing while it's cheap is the kindest version of this you can run.

That's what actually happened for us. We took the awkward five-minute conversation now over the expensive one later. The company being worth nothing yet is what made the conversation free.

If you're splitting equity with a friend, or thinking about it: you don't need a valuation to write down how you'd decide things. You need it more before there's a number than after. Have the talk while it costs nothing.
